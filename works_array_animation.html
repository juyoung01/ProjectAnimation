2D arrays of the working cages — rhombic, square, honeycomb

The three nets, and why only these

A bond translates the neighbour along a corner axis, so the lattice vectors are not free. That leaves two constructions. Rhombic uses pure translates: every cage is the same pose, the partner corner is the antipode, and the bond vector is 2Va. Square and honeycomb use two cages per cell, the partner being an inverted copy (pose −I); the bond is still 2Va but the Bravais vectors become the differences 2(Va − Va₀), which opens up angles the single-orientation lattice cannot reach. Both sublattices sit in one plane up to a fixed buckling offset.

All three nets have only even cycles — rhombic and square are 4-coordinate, honeycomb is 3-coordinate and bipartite — so whatever J comes out, the product around every cycle is +1 and the array can never be frustrated. That is why the frustration criterion, which took up so much of the analysis, never actually binds for a cage that has a working unit. The 6-neighbour triangular net is the one that could frustrate, and no even prism can build it: t₁ + t₂ never lands on a corner axis.

J is not assumed here. For each bond the page forms the world-frame flip increment ΔR = P·R(na, −2σaεθ)·Pᵀ on both sides and finds the ε pairing that makes them equal. The rhombic net inherits J = −σaσā from the antipodal partner, which flips with the prism order; the inverted-partner nets come out uniform.

Scope. Pure rigid-body geometry — no energies, forces, compliance, proteins or dynamics. "Works" means a set of closure equations has exactly two solutions and the fusions survive the switch. Steric self-overlap between non-bonded cages is not checked here, and some corner choices are known to overlap. Arrays built on the released-arm model, where non-bipartite solids also close, are untested — that remains the single largest gap.
